What we know today as early modern philosophy was forged in the opening years of the 17th century, in the writings of such thinkers as Francis Bacon, Thomas Hobbes, and René Descartes. We think of this period as the beginning of modern philosophy in part because these philosophers saw themselves as the vanguard of an intellectual revolution, whose goal was to break with the philosophy of the past. Here they identified their most important target as Aristotle, whose teachings in logic and metaphysics had dominated educated opinion in Europe through most of the previous millennium. Almost all of the best-known philosophers and scientists of the 17th century saw Aristotle’s views as a significant impediment to the advance of knowledge, and believed that progress could only begin once the edifice of Aristotle’s system had been razed and philosophy could begin to rebuild on solid foundations.

The term style is used loosely to mean the distinctive way a thing is done in any field of human endeavor. It is simply a term of praise in most cases: “to have style” means to have distinction or to stand out. But something else is implied, too, when we say that something “has no style.” Such a thing, we feel, is not only undistinguished but also undistinguishable. In other words, we do not know how to classify it, how to put it into its proper context, because it seems to be pointing in several directions at once. In the visual arts, style means the particular way in which the forms that make up any given work of art are chosen and fitted together. To art historians the study of styles is of central importance; it not only enables them to find out, by means of careful analysis and comparison, when and where (and by whom) a given work was produced, but also leads them to understand the artist’s intention as expressed through the style of his work. This intention depends on both the artist’s personality and the setting in which he lives and works. Accordingly, we speak of “period styles” if we are concerned with those features which distinguish, let us say, Egyptian art as a whole from Greek art. And within these broad period styles we in turn distinguish the styles of particular phases, such as the Old Kingdom; or wherever it seems appropriate, we differentiate national or local styles within a period, until we arrive at the personal styles of individual artists.

December 26 is not only a day for Santa Claus to catch his breath; it is also a public holiday known as “Boxing Day” in the United Kingdom and other British Commonwealth countries such as Australia, Canada, and New Zealand. Despite its peculiar name, Boxing Day has nothing to do with fisticuffs, discarded Christmas boxes, or the return of unwanted presents. The term is of British origin, and the Oxford English Dictionary traces its earliest printed use to 1833, four years before Charles Dickens referred to it in “The Pickwick Papers.” The exact roots of the name remain unclear, but there are two leading theories, both of which are connected to __________ on the day after Christmas. One idea suggests that centuries ago, December 26 was the day when lords of the manor and aristocrats typically distributed “Christmas boxes” filled with money, small gifts, and leftovers from Christmas dinner to household servants and employees, who had been required to work on December 25, in recognition of good service throughout the year. Another popular explanation links the name to alms boxes placed in churches during the Advent season, which were opened and distributed to the poor on December 26 — the feast day of St. Stephen, the first Christian martyr and a figure associated with charity. While the tradition of almsgiving on December 26 has largely faded with charity now being given in the weeks leading up to Christmas, the name Boxing Day has endured. Today, the holiday is widely associated with sporting events such as soccer and cricket, shopping, and visiting friends rather than with charitable distribution.

What Fueled the 1983 Cabbage Patch Kid Craze?

원출처 ↗
시험에 실린 본문 읽기
Toy fads have surged and faded for as long as commercial playthings have existed, and Americans have eagerly embraced popular hits such as Barbie dolls, Beanie Babies, and Tickle Me Elmos. Yet few crazes matched the gotta-have-it insanity that followed the release of Cabbage Patch Kids in 1983. This extraordinary demand stemmed not only from savvy marketing but also from the dolls’ unusual origins. Creator Xavier Roberts developed them from handmade “Doll Babies,” presenting his own versions as unique individuals “born” in a cabbage patch and available for “adoption.” When electronics company Coleco licensed and mass-produced the dolls, demand soared far beyond expectations. Although millions were manufactured, production struggled to keep pace, in part because each doll was designed to appear one of a kind, complete with individual features and birth certificates. The dolls’ appeal also reflected broader cultural and social changes. Their diverse appearances and imperfect, homely charm contrasted with the idealized toys that dominated the market, making them feel more relatable. They promoted a message of self-acceptance and were marketed to both boys and girls, cutting across the era’s rigidly gendered toy aisles. As video games like Atari surged in the early 1980s, the dolls likely tapped into a nostalgic yearning for unplugged, old-fashioned play. At the same time, parenting styles in the 1970s and 1980s had become increasingly child-centered, while television advertising intensified children’s desires for specific toys. Many parents felt compelled to satisfy those wishes, reinforcing demand. Together, these factors explain why Cabbage Patch Kids became more than a passing fad, marking a rare and powerful moment in consumer and toy history.
